**MARKET BRIEF — SEPT. 8, 2026**

Friday gave the Fed a number to argue about. The weekend gave the market a problem that number can't measure.

Here's what changed.

CPI measures **August**. Oil is already pricing **September**, in real time, against a supply shock that happened after the data period ended.

We've spent weeks watching this market treat physical disruption as more important than rhetoric. The weekend just produced the strongest physical escalation yet — arriving in the same week as the inflation number supposed to settle the Fed's decision.

So the question isn't whether CPI or the war wins the headline.

It's whether an **August inflation reading can still determine policy when September's inflation risk is being repriced in real time.**

Two other tests matter.

Wednesday's **10-year auction** arrives as Norway has flagged an **$80B** reduction in Treasury holdings, while Treasury continues using buybacks to support the long end.

And gold and Bitcoin enter the week at **0.86 correlation**, a six-year high, after spending most of August moving apart. $BTC is already back below **$79,000**.

If that correlation breaks again during a live war shock, that's a separate signal: different buyers may still be pricing different risks.

Tuesday is about the reopen.

**Can Friday's CPI still set the Fed's path, or did the weekend already start writing the inflation story that CPI is supposed to tell?** 🍌

#######POST 5 afternoon

Oil just got a different kind of shock.

For a week, the market has been pricing the war as a transit problem: whether tankers move through Hormuz, whether $KHG gets hit.

Now the question is whether the barrels themselves are safe to produce.

Houthi rebels struck Saudi Arabia's Jazan region directly, hitting a 400,000-barrel-a-day Aramco refinery, a fuel plant, and infrastructure near a civilian airport. Seventy-three people were wounded, several facilities halted operations, and Brent pushed toward $99 — its highest since late July.

That's a different mechanism.

Hormuz is a route.

Jazan is production capacity, inside a country that isn't a party to the US-Iran exchange.

The market has spent weeks asking whether barrels can leave the region.

Now it has to ask whether the barrels can safely be made.

And those are very different disruptions.

A route disruption can fade once ships move again. Lost production capacity doesn't reverse on the same timeline.

Watch whether Brent holds above $97 once Aramco confirms how much capacity is actually offline.

Not how many facilities paused.

The capacity number tells us whether today's move was a spike — or the beginning of a structural loss. 🍌
