#######POST 6 afternoon

A few hours ago, oil's decline looked like a bet on diplomacy. By now, that explanation looks too small.

By this afternoon, that explanation looks too narrow.

WTI is still falling. Brent is below $92. The threats haven't disappeared, but neither has the selling.

What's becoming clearer is that the market may be separating economic pressure from military escalation.

Sanctions can tighten. Threats can get louder. But as long as neither becomes physical disruption, the market seems willing to treat the risk as containable.

That's different from saying an off-ramp has been found.

An off-ramp is a prediction about how this ends. This is something simpler: so far, oil appears to care far more about what happens physically than what gets said politically.

That gives us a much cleaner test from here.

More sanctions may not be enough to move oil higher. Another round of rhetoric may not either.

But actual disruption — a strike, damaged infrastructure, or a meaningful interruption to flows — would test whether this rule the market has been applying for two days actually holds. 🍌

When the cost of capital eases while the currency weakens, retaliation headlines have to compete with a much bigger force already moving through the market.

We've been watching pieces of that mechanism since Friday. Today was the first time it held together for an entire session.

Tomorrow's PCE print is the real test. If inflation comes in hot and yields still refuse to rise, we'll know this liquidity trade is stronger than the data trying to challenge it.

If yields jump, we'll find out what finally breaks it. 🍌
