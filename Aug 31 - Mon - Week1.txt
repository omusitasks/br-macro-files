**MARKET BRIEF — AUG. 31, 2026**

We start the week with three unfinished trades from last week, and the weekend just opened a fourth.

The first is the curve. Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech pushed September hike odds to roughly **57%**, from about **40%** the week before, and December hike odds above **70%**. The **2-year** moved with it. The **30-year** didn't. Treasury's buyback program has kept the long end contained for two weeks, leaving the split intact: the Fed is repricing one end while the Gorilla managing the debt is leaning against the other. This morning the **10-year sits at 4.72%**, unchanged and still waiting for something to force the break.

Then there's oil. Five straight sessions of declines had the market pricing **Hormuz traffic over Iranian rhetoric — evidence over threats**. Overnight, that changed. The US struck Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island Sunday, targeting mines meant for the strait — the first acknowledged strike in a month. **Brent is back above $90. WTI is near $86.** The mechanism we've watched for two weeks just met the kind of event it was built to explain: not another statement, but an action on the water.

The third is earnings discipline. **$NVDA, $CRM and $CRWD** were rewarded for clearing the bar. **$MRVL and $PYPL** were punished for margin and timeline slippage despite beating. That standard hasn't been tested by a quiet week yet.

Now the calendar puts the labor market under a microscope: **Chicago PMI today, ISM Manufacturing and JOLTS Tuesday, ADP Wednesday, ISM Services Thursday, and Friday's jobs report.** This is the data set capable of unwinding the hike expectations Warsh just priced — the same way one soft payrolls report reset the rate conversation three weeks ago.

The question for this week isn't simply whether the labor market is weakening.

It's whether the data confirms the hike the front end just priced — or whether this weekend's oil escalation ends up mattering more than anything the numbers say. 🍌

#######POST 1 morning

Three days into the oil discount, we said the real test wasn't another threat. It was whether an actual strike would move the market differently.

Overnight, it happened. The US hit Iranian rocket launchers aimed at Hormuz mines — the first acknowledged strike in a month. Tehran immediately fired back, launching retaliatory missile strikes at US bases in Jordan.

Brent is back above $90. Oil funds are up more than 3%. Energy is leading before the open.

And almost everything else is barely moving.

S&P futures are only slightly lower. The 10-year is still near Friday's close. Short-dated Treasuries are actually catching a small bid.

A month of headlines moved oil without taking the rest of the market with it. The first real strike is doing the same thing — so far.

If that gap survives the session, the market isn't dismissing the escalation. It's containing it. 🍌




#######POST 2 midday

Chicago PMI came in at 47.2 against 44.8 expected.

Still contraction. Still weak.

But futures turned green almost immediately.

Brent didn't care. It's still above $90.

That's the interesting part of this morning. The same market is now carrying two completely different stories: oil is still pricing the overnight escalation, while equities just found something else to believe in.

The PMI didn't erase the geopolitical risk. It simply gave stocks a reason to look past it.

If both hold into the close — oil above $90 and equities higher — that's harder to dismiss as one market temporarily ignoring another. 🍌



#######POST 3 midday

The 2-year moved 2 basis points this morning.

The 10-year barely moved at all.

Chicago PMI beat expectations, giving the front end something to price. The long end stayed near 4.71%, almost exactly where Friday left it.

We've now seen this same split four times in nine sessions.

The Fed keeps moving expectations at the short end. Treasury's buyback support keeps the long end anchored.

And this week gives the front end plenty more ammunition: ISM Services Thursday, payrolls Friday.

The question isn't whether the 2-year can move again.

It's whether the 10-year finally decides to move with it. 🍌

The PMI bounce didn't absorb the oil risk. It ran into a rate market that was already leaning the other way.

Now watch the 2-year and 10-year. If the short end keeps moving while the long end stays pinned, Treasury is still winning that argument. 🍌
