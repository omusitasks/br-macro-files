#######MARKET BRIEF SEPT 4, 2026

🌅 MARKET BRIEF — SEPT 11, 2026

The market broke yesterday. This morning, CPI gets to find out whether it broke for real.

For four sessions, the calendar kept knocking.

ADP missed.

ISM Services beat and got overridden by Waller's framing.

Two direct military strikes on U.S. warships left the 10-year where it started.

Then PPI came in hot at 5.4% YoY vs. 5.3% expected, with diesel up 24.1% MoM.

The 10-year barely reacted.

Then Thursday found the threshold.

Houthi forces seized Yemen's Mocha port and pushed toward Bab el-Mandeb, opening a second chokepoint alongside Hormuz.

WTI crossed $100.

Brent reached $105–106.

The 10-year jumped to 4.91–4.93%.

The VIX broke a 28-session range and closed above 17.

The market didn't suddenly become more sensitive to information.

It found information big enough to force a repricing.

Now comes the uncomfortable test.

CPI measures August.

The market is trading September.

August doesn't know about $101+ oil.

It doesn't know about Mocha.

It doesn't know that the Red Sea workaround Saudi Arabia relied on is now under pressure too.

So if CPI comes in hot and the 10-year still refuses to move materially beyond 4.93%, we have our fifth data point.

If CPI moves the tape, that's arguably more important.

It would mean the economic calendar still has enough authority to compete with a market increasingly obsessed with physical supply.

Then there's oil.

Thursday was the first session in this leg where WTI gained faster than Brent — 4.29% vs. 4.12%.

One day isn't a regime change.

But if WTI keeps that lead today, the old regional-chokepoint story gets harder to defend.

And finally: 4.90% and 17.

Those were ceilings.

Thursday broke both.

If the 10-year and VIX stay above them today without another physical shock, yesterday wasn't just a reaction.

It was a repricing.

Gold and Bitcoin are carrying their own disagreement into the morning. Their 90-day correlation touched 0.86 last week. Since then, every serious test has pulled them apart.

So Friday isn't really asking whether the war can escalate again.

We already know the answer.

It's asking something more interesting:

Can the calendar take the market back?

Or did Thursday teach it to look at the water first — and everything else second? 🍌

#######POST 1 morning


**CPI is arriving into a market that already has a position.**

The market is pricing roughly **71% odds of a hike next week** before the number even prints.

So the question isn't whether CPI changes the probability.

It's whether it changes the **price of the position**.

August CPI describes a month that ended before this week's latest oil repricing. This morning, that repricing is already partially unwinding: **WTI -3.35%** and **Brent -3.61%**.

That creates an unusual setup.

A hot CPI would normally strengthen the case for higher yields. But if the 10-year instead waits for oil to establish today's direction before moving, the market is effectively treating current energy prices as more informative than yesterday's inflation data.

And if CPI produces a move that quickly fades while oil continues falling, today's number may be remembered less for what it said about August than for what it failed to change about September.

**8:30 AM is the first test.**

Does the bond market trade the number — or trade through it? 🍌



#######POST 2 morning


**The WTI-Brent spread has a harder job today: give back yesterday's move.**

WTI is down **3.35%**.

Brent is down **3.61%**.

WTI is therefore falling **0.26 percentage points less** than Brent this morning.

That matters because Thursday wasn't just about how high each benchmark went. It changed their relative behavior.

Now the market gets the opposite experiment.

If WTI continues to outperform Brent on the way down, yesterday's convergence has some persistence. The relationship isn't merely reacting when geopolitical risk accelerates.

But if Brent regains the lead decisively and the spread starts separating again, Thursday's move becomes easier to classify as a one-day repricing.

That's the clean test.

Not whether oil falls.

**Whether WTI gives back less of the move than Brent.**

By the close, the spread should tell us whether Thursday changed the relationship — or just the day's price. 🍌


#######POST 3 morning

**The market got two reasons to move the 10-year this morning. It chose neither.**

One force is pushing rates higher.

Another is pulling the inflation impulse back through oil.

Yet the 10-year is still near **4.95%**, roughly where Thursday left it.

That is more revealing than the CPI reaction itself.

Because yesterday established a new level through a physical shock.

Today gave that level a chance to fail.

Hotter inflation didn't push it materially higher.

Oil falling — **Brent -3.6%, WTI -2.2%** — didn't pull it back down either.

So the question has changed.

We're no longer asking whether **4.90%** can be reached.

We're asking whether the market has become comfortable **holding it**.

If 4.95% survives opposing inputs all the way into the close, Thursday's repricing starts looking less like an event and more like a new equilibrium. 🍌


#######POST 4 morning

**Oil is falling. The old relationship isn't.**

Brent is around **$103.78**, down **3.6%**.

WTI is around **$99.23**, down **2.2%**.

The interesting part isn't that both are lower.

It's that WTI is still giving back less.

That distinction matters because yesterday's move was the first time this leg showed WTI outperforming Brent on the way higher.

Now we're watching the same relationship under pressure in the opposite direction.

So far, it is holding.

That makes the Thursday move harder to dismiss as a one-session geopolitical premium.

A regime change doesn't need oil to keep rising.

It needs the relationship between the two benchmarks to survive when the shock starts coming out of the price.

If WTI continues to fall less than Brent into the close, the market is telling us the second route is still being priced as structurally relevant. 🍌


#######POST 5 morning

**The dollar is becoming the awkward part of the rates story.**

A market can price a much higher probability of tighter policy and still refuse to reward the currency that should benefit from it.

That's what we're watching with **DXY near 99**.

The important comparison isn't today's CPI headline.

It's the persistence of the disagreement.

Rates can reprice hawkishly.

The dollar can remain stubbornly flat.

And when that happens alongside oil reversing while the 10-year holds near **4.95%**, the usual chain — inflation → Fed → yields → dollar — is no longer transmitting cleanly.

Something else is sitting between those links.

The September 2 episode gave us an early version of this divergence. Today's tape gives us a harder test because the policy repricing is now much larger, with hike odds around **90%**.

If DXY still refuses to respond into the afternoon, the question isn't why the dollar didn't rally.

It's **what is absorbing the policy signal before it reaches the currency.**🍌



#######POST 6 midday


**The inflation problem just got harder to blame on oil.**

Headline CPI did what it was supposed to do: **0.4% MoM, 3.4% YoY**.

The market's reaction came from somewhere else.

Core CPI ran **0.3% vs. 0.2% expected**, while the annual core rate stayed at **2.4%**.

That distinction matters.

A hot core reading can't be explained away by **$103 Brent** or **$99 WTI**. Those prices aren't in the core basket.

So for the first time in this stretch, the market has a hotter inflation signal coming from somewhere outside the obvious war-energy channel.

That doesn't make one month a trend.

But it creates a problem for the easiest policy narrative.

If next month's core print also beats by **0.1 percentage point**, the Fed has a much harder time treating the inflation pressure as merely energy passing through the economy.

Today's number isn't the story.

**Whether it repeats is.** 🍌



#######POST 7 midday


**The 10-year has stopped needing oil to defend the level.**

Brent is down **3.6%**.

WTI is down **2.2%**.

Yet the 10-year remains near **4.95%**, essentially where Thursday's repricing left it.

That changes the equation.

For weeks, the cleanest explanation for the yield's behavior was that oil had become the market's trigger.

Now oil is moving the other way.

And the yield isn't following.

At the same time, core CPI has given rates a second source of support that doesn't depend on the energy market at all.

That's the development worth watching into the close.

If Brent continues lower while the 10-year remains above **4.90%**, the market is demonstrating that today's rate level can survive an oil reversal.

Oil may have been the catalyst for the move.

It may no longer be the reason the level stays there. 🍌



#######POST 8 afternoon


**The 10-year just broke a ceiling while almost everything else was celebrating.**

Thursday's high was **4.989%**.

This afternoon: **4.992%**.

New multi-year high.

But look at what surrounded it.

Stocks are up more than **1%**.

Oil is lower.

The fresh yield high arrived without another physical shock — and with the oil impulse actually moving in the opposite direction.

Then the 10-year settled back near **4.95%**, roughly where it opened.

That combination is more interesting than the headline high itself.

The market is comfortable buying equities on cheaper oil while simultaneously demanding a higher ceiling from long-duration debt.

So Thursday's question has changed.

It wasn't whether **4.90%** would survive once the trigger disappeared.

It did.

Now the market is testing whether **4.95%** becomes the level it refuses to give back.

If it survives into the close, next week's Fed decision arrives with the Treasury curve already having moved the goalposts. 🍌


#######POST 9 afternoon

**The US bond market may be discovering that this isn't a US-only problem.**

Germany's 10-year crossed **3.5%**, its first time since **April 2011**.

Japan's yield moved higher again.

Australia added **12bps**.

South Korea added **8bps**.

Different central banks.

Different economies.

Same direction in long-duration yields.

That makes the US 10-year's **4.992%** print harder to isolate as a Treasury-versus-Fed story.

The ECB already hiked Thursday, while officials are warning that persistent energy costs could require more.

If oil is forcing a simultaneous repricing across government bond markets, a larger US buyback can influence the US curve without solving the pressure coming from elsewhere.

That's the bigger question heading into next week's FOMC.

Does the Fed move the US curve?

Or is the US curve now moving as part of something much larger? 🍌


#######MARKET CLOSE/CENSUS POST

MARKET STRUCTURE CENSUS — SEPT. 11, 2026

**The week ended with the market telling two different stories at the same time.**

Core inflation ran hotter.

The 10-year reached a level not seen since before the 2008 crisis.

And the S&P 500 had its strongest session in three weeks.

By the close, all three were true.

August core CPI came in at **0.3% vs. 0.2% expected**, while headline CPI landed exactly at **0.4% MoM and 3.4% YoY** and annual core held at **2.4%**. Hike odds jumped to roughly **90%**.

Then oil started giving some of Thursday's shock back.

Brent settled down **2.8% to $104.61**.

WTI fell **2.4% to $100.05**.

Iranian state media said Tehran would meet Gulf states in Oman to discuss the Strait of Hormuz.

The 10-year absorbed all of it.

It touched **4.992%**, clearing the **4.989%** ceiling that had stood since October 2023, before closing near **4.92%**.

That's the part of the week worth carrying forward.

The yield didn't need another physical shock to make a new high.

It also didn't surrender the level when the original oil trigger started reversing.

It moved higher, gave some of it back, and still finished near the level Thursday's repricing had established.

The market isn't waiting for one clean explanation anymore.

It's absorbing contradictory signals and finding roughly the same destination.

Stocks chose a different destination today.

The S&P rose **0.86% to 7,656.98**.

The Dow gained **509 points to 52,573.29**.

The Nasdaq added **0.96%**, snapping four straight losing sessions.

The **VIX** fell more than **11% to 15.84**, dropping back through both **16.34** and **17** after breaking above those levels Thursday for the first time in **28 sessions**.

Equities said relief.

Volatility said relief.

The 10-year didn't.

That divergence is the image I want to leave this week with.

Oil gave us the clearest structural change.

Brent finished the week **+8.7%**.

WTI finished **+9.4%**.

For the first time in this leg of the war, WTI outpaced Brent **on a weekly basis**, not merely intraday.

Thursday's Mocha port seizure and the push toward Bab el-Mandeb exposed a second route alongside Hormuz.

Friday then tested the relationship without another escalation.

It held.

That retires the old regional-chokepoint framing we've been tracking since August.

Not because oil stayed at its highs.

Because the relationship survived the reversal.

Then there's **$BTC**.

It finished the week down roughly **4%**, near **$77,000**.

More importantly, price and ETF flows finally stopped arguing.

ETF flows are on pace to end a **three-week inflow streak** with nearly **$500M in net outflows through Thursday**.

For weeks, one told us accumulation while the other told us weakness.

This time, they agreed.

So the week closes with several questions answered — but not the one that matters most.

Oil found a second chokepoint.

The 10-year found a higher floor.

Stocks found relief.

The VIX gave back its breakout.

$BTC finally stopped contradicting its own flows.

And yet the Fed heads into next week with roughly **90% hike odds** while the two biggest parts of the market — bonds and equities — are behaving as if they are pricing different worlds.

That's where we leave it.

The screens go dark for the weekend.

The war doesn't.

The inflation data doesn't.

And the bond market isn't finished arguing with the equity market.

Next week brings the FOMC.

By then, one of these two signals will have to prove it was reading the room correctly.

Have a good weekend. 🍌
